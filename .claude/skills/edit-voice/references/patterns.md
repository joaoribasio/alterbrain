# Editing principles and patterns to cut

The rules for `/edit-voice`. The SKILL.md says when to use them. Quoted example phrases below are examples of tells to remove, not text to write.

## Editing principles

- **Keep the writer's real voice.** First notice the draft's vocabulary, rhythm, bluntness, humour, uncertainty, digressions and level of polish. Keep the traits that feel personal. Do not make every paragraph equally tidy. Do not rewrite distinctive lines for consistency.
- **Make the minimum effective edit.** Fix AI patterns, errors, repetition and unclear passages. Leave strong human sentences alone. A rough draft with a real voice should still sound like the same person afterwards.
- **Lead with the point when the set-up adds nothing.** Cut generic throat-clearing. Keep a personal aside, story or admission when it gives context, tension or character.
- **Front-load only when it helps.** Put conclusions early when that makes the text clearer. Do not force every section into the same point-detail-background shape.
- **Keep the user's meaning.** Do not invent claims, examples, numbers or opinions. If something is unclear, ask.
- **Open it up, do not dumb it down.** Keep the substance and precision. Strip out only what makes it hard to read: jargon, long sentences, abstract nouns, tangled structure.
- **Use active voice.** "The team shipped it Tuesday" beats "the decision emerged". Things should not do human verbs.
- **Make every sentence earn its place.** Cut empty qualifiers. Keep "I think", "maybe" or "to be honest" when they express real uncertainty or the writer's speaking rhythm.
- **Untangle without flattening the rhythm.** Split a sentence only when it is genuinely hard to follow. Keep longer spoken sentences, fragments and changes of pace when they are clear and typical of the writer.
- **Be concrete.** "The integration improved efficiency" becomes "The integration cut deploy time from 40 minutes to 4", but only if the draft gives that fact. Never add the number yourself.
- **Use the portability test.** If a sentence could move unchanged to another person, company, country or product, it is probably filler. Cut it, or replace it with a fact, example or judgement from the draft that belongs to this subject.
- **Show, do not tell the reader what to think.** Cut commentary that labels a point important, surprising or obvious. If the text around it already shows the point, delete the label.
- **Protect the specific fact.** Do not smooth a useful detail into a general claim.
- **Make verbs do the work.** "Made a decision" becomes "decided". "Has the ability to" becomes "can".
- **Know the job.** Before touching structure or words, know what the text is for and who reads it.
- **Keep useful edge.** Keep strong opinions, blunt language, humour, self-interruptions and honest admissions when they belong to the writer. Do not swap them for safer wording.
- **Keep the structure unless it hurts.** If you reorganise, say why in the "What changed" list.

## Words to cut

**Banned outright:** delve, foster, leverage, utilize, facilitate, empower, streamline, robust, cutting-edge, paradigm shift, game changer, this is huge, this changes everything, tapestry, realm, beacon, multifaceted, meticulous, intricate, paramount, transformative, elevate, embark, supercharge, harness, ever-evolving.

**Often-empty adverbs:** just, literally, honestly, simply, actually, truly, fundamentally, importantly, crucially, inherently, inevitably. Cut when they add nothing. Keep when they carry emphasis, doubt, contrast or the writer's spoken rhythm.

**Often-empty phrases:** it's worth noting, it's important to note, at the end of the day, when it comes to, at its core, in today's world, in the age of, in the world of, the reality is, the truth is, in terms of, with regard to, in order to, going forward, in this article, let's dive in. Cut when they delay the point. Keep an occasional one if it is part of the writer's recognisable voice and the sentence still earns its place.

## Patterns to cut

**Binary contrasts.** "This is not X. It's Y." / "The question isn't X, it's Y." / "It's not just X but Y." State Y directly.

**Throat-clearing openers.** "Here's the thing", "Let me be clear", "I'll be honest", "The uncomfortable truth is". Cut and state the point.

**Faux-insight set-ups.** "This is the part most people skip", "What most people get wrong", "Here's what nobody tells you". They flatter the writer as the lone expert. Cut the set-up and let the claim stand.

**Colon reveals.** A noun phrase, a colon, then a dramatic reveal: "The best part: it learns." Write a plain sentence. Use colons for lists, labels and quotes. After a colon use sentence case unless grammar, a proper noun, a title or code requires otherwise.

**Superficial analysis.** Trailing "-ing" clauses that pretend to explain meaning: "highlighting", "underscoring", "reflecting", "showcasing". Replace with the actual consequence, or cut.

**Importance puffery.** "Stands as a testament", "marks a pivotal moment", "plays a vital role", "underscores its significance". State the fact. Let the reader judge.

**Interpretive metadiscourse.** Lines that tell the reader what to notice or how to read: "That last part matters more than it sounds", "The key point is", "As you can see", "This distinction matters", redundant "In other words". If the point is clear, delete. Otherwise replace with support from the draft.

**Weasel attribution.** "Experts agree", "studies show", "many argue", "widely regarded as". Name the source or cut the claim. If the user has no source, ask. Never invent one.

**Fake-strong verbs.** "Serves as a centralised hub" becomes "is" or the plain verb for what it does. Prefer "is" and "has" when clearer.

**Synonym cycling.** If the clear word is right, repeat it. Do not rotate terms for style.

**Negative listing.** "Not a X. Not a Y. A Z." Say Z.

**Dramatic fragmentation.** "X. And Y. And Z." or "That's it. That's the whole thing." Use full sentences.

**Robotic rhythm.** Repeated sentence shapes, identical paragraph structures, stacked punchy fragments. Vary the shape only when it helps the point.

**Rhetorical set-ups.** "What if I told you...", "Think about it:", "Plot twist:", and self-answered "Question? Answer." pairs. Drop them and make the point.

**Fake-profound kickers.** A final line that turns the point into a cute metaphor or mic-drop. Do not rewrite it into a better one. Delete it and end on the clearest concrete sentence already there. If the ending needs closure, add a plain takeaway or next action.

**Summary-recap endings.** "In conclusion", "Ultimately", "Overall", or a last paragraph that repeats the text. End on the last concrete point or next action.

**Formatting slop.** Emoji in headings, bold scattered mid-sentence, bullet lists where two sentences would read better, headings over two-sentence sections. Format should follow content.

**Em dashes.** Not a default rhythm tool. In short copy use none. In longer drafts one or two are fine if they clearly beat commas, full stops or brackets. Remove clusters and decorative dashes.

## Languages other than English

The word and phrase lists above are English. For another language, apply only the language-neutral rules: structure (binary contrasts, set-ups, recap endings, kickers, dramatic fragments), rhythm, formatting, em dashes, concreteness, and the user's own profile. Say plainly that coverage is thinner in that language.
