# Porter's Generic Strategies

**Definition:** A firm can win in three basic ways: be the lowest-cost producer, offer something clearly different (differentiation), or serve one narrow segment very well (focus).

## When to use / when not to
- Use it to test whether a firm's strategy is clear and consistent.
- Use it to explain why a firm is "stuck in the middle".
- Do not use it as a full strategy. It gives a direction, not a plan.
- Do not treat the boxes as rigid. Some firms combine cost and differentiation, but it is hard.

## Inputs you need
- The firm's cost position against rivals.
- What customers value and will pay extra for.
- The size and needs of the target segment (broad or narrow).

## Step-by-step
1. Name the competitive scope: broad market or narrow segment.
2. Name the source of advantage: lower cost or a differentiated offer.
3. Place the firm in one of four boxes: cost leadership, differentiation, cost focus, differentiation focus.
4. List the activities that support that choice (see [[Value Chain]]).
5. Check for fit: does every major decision (price, product, hiring) support the choice?
6. Check the risks: can rivals copy it, or can customers stop caring?

## Common pitfalls (what professors mark down)
- Claiming a firm is both cheapest and best with no evidence of how.
- Confusing cost leadership with a low price. Cost leadership is about low cost; price is a choice.
- Calling any small firm "focus" when it only has a small share.
- Ignoring "stuck in the middle", the classic failure.
- Naming a strategy without showing the activities that deliver it.

## Worked example (synthetic)
Two Dutch bike makers:
- **Orange Cycles** makes one basic model in large volumes. Unit cost is EUR 180 against an industry average of EUR 220. It sells at EUR 260. This is **cost leadership**.
- **Lumen Bikes** builds custom e-bikes for city commuters at EUR 1,800. Customers pay for design and service. This is **differentiation focus**.

A third firm sells mid-priced bikes with mid-level quality and no clear edge. It is stuck in the middle, and it loses to both.

## Questions to ask yourself
- Where exactly does the cost advantage or the uniqueness come from?
- Would customers notice if we dropped it?
- What would a rival need to copy it?
- Are we trying to do two things at once?
