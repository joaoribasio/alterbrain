# SWOT

**Definition:** A two-by-two list. Strengths and weaknesses are inside the firm. Opportunities and threats are outside it.

## When to use / when not to
- Use it as a quick summary at the end of your analysis.
- Use it to start a team discussion.
- Do not use it as your analysis. It only sums up what you found with other tools.
- Do not use it without a decision to make. A SWOT with no "so what?" scores low.

## Inputs you need
- Internal findings: [[Value Chain]], [[VRIO]], financials.
- External findings: [[PESTEL]], [[Porter's Five Forces]], customer data.

## Step-by-step
1. Do the deeper analysis first (external and internal).
2. List 3 to 5 items per box. Keep each one specific and evidence-based.
3. Check each item sits in the right box (inside the firm, or outside it).
4. Rank items by importance. Drop the weak ones.
5. Cross them (this is sometimes called a TOWS matrix):
   - Strengths + Opportunities: how to grow.
   - Strengths + Threats: how to defend.
   - Weaknesses + Opportunities: what to fix to take the chance.
   - Weaknesses + Threats: what to avoid.
6. Finish with two or three clear priorities.

## Common pitfalls (what professors mark down)
- Long, vague lists ("good brand", "strong team").
- Putting external facts in the internal boxes (for example, "high competition" as a weakness).
- Strengths that are not strengths against rivals.
- No ranking and no link to a decision.
- Treating one fact as both an opportunity and a threat without explaining.

## Worked example (synthetic)
Alex Doe is deciding whether to start a student coffee cart in Rotterdam.

| | Helpful | Harmful |
|---|---|---|
| Inside | **S:** prime campus pitch agreed; low start-up cost (EUR 3,000) | **W:** no food-safety certificate yet; one person only |
| Outside | **O:** 2,000 students, no rival on site | **T:** university may open its own cafe |

Cross: use the pitch (S) and the missing rival (O) to start in term one. Get the certificate first (W). Sign a one-year agreement to reduce the university threat (T).

## Questions to ask yourself
- Is each item backed by evidence?
- Is each strength a strength compared with rivals?
- Which two items matter most?
- What decision does this lead to?
