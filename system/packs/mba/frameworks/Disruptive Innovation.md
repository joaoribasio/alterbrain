# Disruptive Innovation

**Definition:** A firm with fewer resources enters at the bottom or in a new corner of a market with a simpler, cheaper or more convenient product, improves over time, and then wins the mainstream from the leaders.

## When to use / when not to
- Use it to explain why strong firms lose to small newcomers.
- Use it to judge whether a new entrant is a real threat or only a rival.
- Do not call every new technology "disruptive". Many are only "sustaining" (better for the same customers).
- Do not use it as a forecast. Many low-end entrants fail.

## Inputs you need
- The leaders' customers, profits and how they improve products.
- The entrant's offer, cost model and target customers.
- The trend in what mainstream customers need versus what products give.

## Step-by-step
1. Describe the leaders: product, customers, profit model.
2. Describe the entrant's offer. Is it simpler, cheaper, or more convenient?
3. Classify the entry:
   - **Low-end disruption:** serves over-served customers at lower cost and price.
   - **New-market disruption:** serves non-consumers who had no solution.
4. Check the leaders' incentives: do they ignore the entrant because it looks unprofitable?
5. Check the improvement path: will the entrant get good enough for the mainstream?
6. Predict the leaders' options (ignore, copy, buy, set up a separate unit).
7. Advise: how to respond or how to attack.

## Common pitfalls (what professors mark down)
- Calling a better, higher-priced product "disruptive".
- Ignoring that the entrant starts with a low-end or new market, not the mainstream.
- Skipping the improvement path (the "later overtake" part).
- Treating the theory as always right, with no mention of critiques (for example King and Baatartogtokh, 2015).
- No advice for the leader.

## Worked example (synthetic)
Video calls versus in-person language courses:
- **Leaders:** language schools charging EUR 40 an hour in city centres.
- **Entrant:** an app with live tutors at EUR 12 an hour, aimed at people who never had time or money for classes (**new-market**) and at casual learners (**low-end**).
- **Path:** the app adds speaking practice and exam prep over three years. Quality gets good enough for most learners.
- **Leaders' incentive:** they ignore it as low-margin.
- **Advice:** a school should launch its own online unit, separate from the main business, and keep in-person courses for business clients who pay for premium service.

## Questions to ask yourself
- Is the entrant really cheaper or simpler, or just new?
- Does it start where leaders do not want to go?
- Will it improve fast enough?
- What can the leader do before it is too late?
